Inflation

Inflation is a period of accelerated expansion in the early universe that explains the observed large-scale homogeneity and isotropy.

The Horizon Problem

Standard FRW cosmology predicts causally disconnected regions in the early universe, yet the CMB shows almost identical temperatures everywhere.

Causal limit: distance = c × time

Conformal Time

\( ds^2 = a^2(\tau)(-d\tau^2 + d\chi^2) \)

\( \tau = \int \frac{dt}{a(t)} \)

Light travels at 45° in conformal coordinates: \( d\chi = \pm d\tau \)

Cosmological Horizons

Particle horizon:

\( \chi_{ph} = \int_0^t \frac{dt'}{a(t')} \)

Event horizon:

\( \chi_{eh} = \int_t^\infty \frac{dt'}{a(t')} \)

Comoving Hubble Radius

\( (aH)^{-1} \)

Inflation requires: \( \frac{d}{dt}(aH)^{-1} < 0 \)

This is equivalent to accelerated expansion: \( \ddot{a} > 0 \)

CMB Uniformity

Temperature fluctuations: \( \Delta T / T \sim 10^{-5} \)

Standard cosmology implies ~\(10^9\) causally disconnected regions on the sky.

Solving the Horizon Problem

Inflation stretches space so that regions now far apart were once in causal contact.

Conformal time becomes extremely large during inflation, allowing thermal equilibrium.

60 e-Fold Requirement

The amount of expansion is measured by: \( N = \ln(a_f/a_i) \)

Successful inflation requires: \( N \gtrsim 60 \)

This ensures that observable CMB modes originate from a single causally connected patch.

Quantum Computing & Linear Algebra

    These notes review the core concepts of linear algebra and build toward the mathematical framework used in quantum computing and quantum mechanics. The focus is on vector spaces, matrices, linear transformations, and their role in describing quantum systems.

    Systems of Linear Equations

    A linear equation in multiple variables represents a constraint of the form:

    a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aₙxₙ = b

    A system of such equations can be written compactly as:

    A x = b

    where A is a matrix, x is the vector of unknowns, and b is the output vector.

    Matrices

    A matrix is a rectangular arrangement of numbers used to represent linear transformations and systems of equations.

    A = [aᵢⱼ]

    Matrix multiplication is defined as:

    (AB)ᵢⱼ = Σₖ Aᵢₖ Bₖⱼ

    Key Properties

    • Addition is commutative and associative
    • Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative
    • Identity and inverse matrices play a central role in transformations

    Solution of Linear Systems

    • Consistent: at least one solution exists
    • Inconsistent: no solution exists

    Vectors

    Vectors represent physical quantities with magnitude and direction.

    Vector Spaces

    Vector spaces form the foundation of linear algebra and quantum theory.
