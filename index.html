Inflation & Early Universe Cosmology

Inflation is a period of accelerated expansion proposed to have occurred in the very early universe. It provides elegant solutions to several problems of standard Big Bang cosmology, including the horizon problem, flatness problem, and the absence of unwanted relics. It also explains the origin of the primordial fluctuations that later grew into galaxies and large-scale structure.

Introduction

The observed universe is remarkably homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. However, according to standard FLRW cosmology, distant regions of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) should never have been in causal contact. Inflation proposes a brief era of exponential expansion that stretched a tiny, causally connected region to scales much larger than the observable universe.

The Horizon Problem

The standard Big Bang model predicts that the early universe contained many causally disconnected regions. Nevertheless, the CMB exhibits nearly identical temperatures across the sky, with fluctuations of only one part in one hundred thousand.

This apparent contradiction is known as the Horizon Problem and serves as one of the strongest motivations for inflation.

Slow-Roll Inflation

Inflation is typically driven by a scalar field known as the inflaton. During slow roll, the potential energy of the field dominates over its kinetic energy:

ρϕ ≈ V(ϕ)

The slow evolution of the field produces an approximately constant Hubble parameter and an era of accelerated expansion.

Number of e-Folds

The duration of inflation is measured using the number of e-folds, N. One e-fold corresponds to the scale factor increasing by a factor of e.

N = ln(af/ai)

Successful inflation generally requires more than sixty e-folds of expansion in order to solve the horizon and flatness problems.

Chaotic Inflation

One of the simplest inflationary models is the quadratic potential:

V(ϕ) = ½m²ϕ²

For this model, the slow-roll parameters become:

εV = ηV = 2(MPl/ϕ)²

Inflation occurs while εV < 1, implying:

ϕ > √2 MPl

The largest observable CMB scales are generated approximately sixty e-folds before the end of inflation.

Reheating

When inflation ends, the slow-roll conditions break down and the inflaton rapidly rolls toward the minimum of its potential. The universe at this stage contains very little ordinary matter or radiation.

A mechanism is therefore required to convert the energy stored in the inflaton into ordinary particles. This process is known as reheating.

Scalar Field Oscillations

Near the minimum of its potential, the inflaton can be approximated by:

V(ϕ) ≈ ½m²ϕ²

The field then obeys the Klein–Gordon equation:

ϕ̈ + 3Hϕ̇ + m²ϕ = 0

This equation describes a damped harmonic oscillator, where the expansion of the universe provides the damping term.

Once m ≫ H, the inflaton oscillates rapidly around the minimum of its potential. Averaging over many oscillations yields:

ρϕ ∝ a-3

This scaling is identical to pressureless matter. Consequently, the oscillating inflaton behaves like a dust component with an average pressure close to zero.

⟨Pϕ⟩ ≈ 0

As the inflaton decays into ordinary particles, its energy is transferred into a thermal bath of radiation. This marks the completion of reheating and the beginning of the standard hot Big Bang evolution.

Quantum Computing & Linear Algebra

    These notes review the core concepts of linear algebra and build toward the mathematical framework used in quantum computing and quantum mechanics. The focus is on vector spaces, matrices, linear transformations, and their role in describing quantum systems.

    Systems of Linear Equations

    A linear equation in multiple variables represents a constraint of the form:

    a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aₙxₙ = b

    A system of such equations can be written compactly as:

    A x = b

    where A is a matrix, x is the vector of unknowns, and b is the output vector.

    Matrices

    A matrix is a rectangular arrangement of numbers used to represent linear transformations and systems of equations.

    A = [aᵢⱼ]

    Matrix multiplication is defined as:

    (AB)ᵢⱼ = Σₖ Aᵢₖ Bₖⱼ

    Key Properties

    • Addition is commutative and associative
    • Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative
    • Identity and inverse matrices play a central role in transformations

    Solution of Linear Systems

    A system A x = b may be:

    • Consistent: at least one solution exists
    • Inconsistent: no solution exists

    A homogeneous system A x = 0 always has at least the trivial solution x = 0.

    Vectors

    In physics, vectors represent quantities with magnitude and direction. In mathematics, vectors are abstract elements of vector spaces.

    A system of equations can also be viewed geometrically as a linear combination:

    x a₁ + y a₂ = b

    meaning we express a vector as a combination of basis vectors.

    Vector Spaces

    A vector space consists of:

    • A set V
    • Vector addition
    • Scalar multiplication
    • A field F

    These structures form the foundation of linear algebra and quantum theory.

    Why This Matters in Quantum Computing

    Quantum states are represented as vectors in a Hilbert space, and quantum operations are represented as matrices acting on these vectors. This makes linear algebra the mathematical backbone of quantum computing.
