Inflation & Early Universe Cosmology

    The Horizon Problem

    The Friedmann–Robertson–Walker (FRW) cosmology successfully describes an expanding universe, but it cannot explain why the universe appears so homogeneous and isotropic today. According to the standard Big Bang model, the early universe consisted of many causally disconnected regions that could not exchange information. Despite this, observations of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) reveal nearly identical temperatures and densities across the sky. This contradiction is known as the Horizon Problem.

    Size of a Causal Patch

    A causal patch is a region in which all points can communicate with one another. Since no signal can travel faster than light, the size of such a region is limited by the distance light can travel during a given interval of time.

    Conformal Time

    In cosmology, it is often useful to replace physical time with conformal time, defined as:

    τ = ∫ dt / a(t)

    This transformation makes photon trajectories appear as straight 45° lines on spacetime diagrams, simplifying the causal structure of spacetime.

    Particle Horizon

    The particle horizon is the maximum distance from which light could have reached an observer since the beginning of the universe. It defines the boundary of the observable universe at a given time.

    Event Horizon

    The event horizon represents the farthest distance from which signals emitted now or in the future will ever be received. Unlike the particle horizon, it concerns future observability.

    The Hubble Radius

    The comoving Hubble radius, (aH)-1, characterizes the scale over which causal interactions can occur at a given time. During standard radiation and matter dominated expansion, this scale increases with time.

    Why the CMB is So Uniform

    Around 380,000 years after the Big Bang, electrons and protons combined to form neutral hydrogen. Photons then decoupled from matter and began traveling freely. These photons are observed today as the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB).

    The CMB is extremely uniform, with temperature fluctuations of only about one part in 100,000. However, standard cosmology shows that many regions of the CMB sky were never in causal contact before decoupling, yet they exhibit nearly identical temperatures.

    This contradiction is known as the Horizon Problem and is one of the key motivations for cosmic inflation.

    Reheating

    After inflation ends, the universe is left in a peculiar state. Although the rapid expansion successfully explains the observed homogeneity and isotropy of the universe, it also dilutes any pre-existing matter and radiation. Consequently, the universe must undergo a process that repopulates it with ordinary particles and restores the hot conditions required by the standard Big Bang model.

    During inflation, the dominant contribution to the energy density comes from the inflaton field:

    ρϕ ≈ V(ϕ)

    Inflation ends when the slow-roll conditions break down, typically when the first slow-roll parameter approaches unity:

    ε → 1

    At this stage the inflaton can no longer evolve slowly along its potential. The field gains kinetic energy, accelerated expansion ceases, and the universe exits the inflationary phase.

    The Post-Inflation Universe

    Immediately after inflation, very little ordinary matter or radiation remains. Most of the energy of the universe is still stored in the inflaton field itself. Therefore a mechanism is required to transfer this energy into standard particles.

    This process is known as reheating.

    Scalar Field Oscillations

    After inflation, the inflaton rolls toward the minimum of its potential and begins oscillating around it. Near the minimum, the potential can often be approximated by a quadratic form:

    V(ϕ) ≈ ½m²ϕ²

    The evolution of the field is then governed by the Klein–Gordon equation:

    ϕ̈ + 3Hϕ̇ + m²ϕ = 0

    This equation is mathematically identical to that of a damped harmonic oscillator. The term 3Hϕ̇ acts as a friction term arising from the expansion of the universe.

    Soon after inflation ends, the oscillation frequency becomes much larger than the Hubble expansion rate:

    m ≫ H

    The inflaton therefore oscillates many times during a single Hubble time. Averaging over many oscillations gives:

    ρϕ ∝ a-3

    This is the same scaling law followed by pressureless matter. Consequently, the oscillating inflaton behaves like a dust component with an average pressure close to zero:

    ⟨Pϕ⟩ ≈ 0

    The universe therefore enters a temporary matter-dominated era driven by coherent inflaton oscillations. As the inflaton decays into ordinary particles, its energy is transferred to a thermal bath of radiation, marking the completion of reheating and the beginning of the standard hot Big Bang evolution.

Quantum Computing & Linear Algebra

    These notes review the core concepts of linear algebra and build toward the mathematical framework used in quantum computing and quantum mechanics. The focus is on vector spaces, matrices, linear transformations, and their role in describing quantum systems.

    Systems of Linear Equations

    A linear equation in multiple variables represents a constraint of the form:

    a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aₙxₙ = b

    A system of such equations can be written compactly as:

    A x = b

    where A is a matrix, x is the vector of unknowns, and b is the output vector.

    Matrices

    A matrix is a rectangular arrangement of numbers used to represent linear transformations and systems of equations.

    A = [aᵢⱼ]

    Matrix multiplication is defined as:

    (AB)ᵢⱼ = Σₖ Aᵢₖ Bₖⱼ

    Key Properties

    • Addition is commutative and associative
    • Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative
    • Identity and inverse matrices play a central role in transformations

    Solution of Linear Systems

    A system A x = b may be:

    • Consistent: at least one solution exists
    • Inconsistent: no solution exists

    A homogeneous system A x = 0 always has at least the trivial solution x = 0.

    Vectors

    In physics, vectors represent quantities with magnitude and direction. In mathematics, vectors are abstract elements of vector spaces.

    A system of equations can also be viewed geometrically as a linear combination:

    x a₁ + y a₂ = b

    meaning we express a vector as a combination of basis vectors.

    Vector Spaces

    A vector space consists of:

    • A set V
    • Vector addition
    • Scalar multiplication
    • A field F

    These structures form the foundation of linear algebra and quantum theory.

    Why This Matters in Quantum Computing

    Quantum states are represented as vectors in a Hilbert space, and quantum operations are represented as matrices acting on these vectors. This makes linear algebra the mathematical backbone of quantum computing.
